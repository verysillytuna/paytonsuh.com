**TL;DR.** Nothing new in the last two days could be confirmed: the AI-generated claims from October are still in expert review, and the zero-free half-plane result already had [its own entry](/digest/2026-10-09-zero-free-half-plane/). So, a recent classic from down the hall. The **bunkbed conjecture**, posed by P. W. Kasteleyn in 1985, says that in a "bunkbed" graph with random edges, a vertex is at least as likely to be connected to a vertex on its own floor as to that vertex's copy on the other floor. It sounds too obvious to need proof. In October 2024, Nikita Gladkov, Igor Pak (both UCLA) and Aleksandr Zimin posted [a counterexample](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.02545): a planar graph with 7,222 vertices. It appeared in [PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2420725122) in 2025. Their construction adapts Lawrence Hollom's 2024 counterexample to a version of the conjecture for 3-uniform hypergraphs.

## The question

Take a finite graph $G$ with vertex set $V$. The **bunkbed graph** has two copies of every vertex, $v_0$ downstairs and $v_1$ upstairs. Each edge $uv$ of $G$ appears twice, as $u_0v_0$ and $u_1v_1$, and each vertex gets a vertical post $v_0v_1$.

Now do **percolation**: keep each edge independently with probability $p$ and delete it otherwise. Write $x \leftrightarrow y$ when some path of kept edges joins $x$ and $y$.

> **Bunkbed conjecture (Kasteleyn, 1985).** For every graph $G$, every $p \in [0,1]$ and all vertices $u, v$,
> $$\Pr(u_0 \leftrightarrow v_0) \;\ge\; \Pr(u_0 \leftrightarrow v_1).$$

The intuition: to reach $v_1$ from $u_0$ you have to climb a post somewhere, and climbing should only cost you. There is a second standard form, where the posts at a chosen set of vertices are always present and the others are absent, and only the floor edges are random. The counterexample is stated in that form, with posts at three vertices and $p = \tfrac12$.

## Two small cases by hand

**One edge.** Let $G$ be a single edge $uv$. The bunkbed is a 4-cycle $u_0 - v_0 - v_1 - u_1 - u_0$; take $p = \tfrac12$.

- $u_0 \leftrightarrow v_0$: either the edge $u_0v_0$ is kept (probability $\tfrac12$), or it isn't and the other three edges of the cycle all are ($\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac18$). So $\Pr = \tfrac{9}{16}$.
- $u_0 \leftrightarrow v_1$: the two paths $u_0 v_0 v_1$ and $u_0 u_1 v_1$ share no edge, and each survives with probability $\tfrac14$. So $\Pr = 1 - \left(\tfrac34\right)^2 = \tfrac{7}{16}$.

The conjecture holds, with room to spare.

**A path of length two.** For $G = u - w - v$, the same kind of count (or a $2^{7}$-case enumeration, which is how I checked it) gives $\Pr(u_0 \leftrightarrow v_0) = \tfrac{11}{32}$ and $\Pr(u_0 \leftrightarrow v_1) = \tfrac{10}{32}$ at $p = \tfrac12$. The gap is already smaller.

I also ran an exact enumeration over every graph on three or four vertices, every ordered pair of distinct vertices $u, v$, at $p = \tfrac12$: 798 cases, and the inequality held in all of them. That matches the literature. The conjecture had been proved for several families, among them complete graphs, complete bipartite graphs and wheels, so any counterexample was going to be large and unusual.

## How it was broken

The route went through hypergraphs. In a 3-uniform hypergraph, an "edge" is a triple of vertices, and percolation keeps or deletes whole triples. Hollom found a small hypergraph for which the bunkbed inequality fails. That doesn't directly say anything about graphs, and the authors note that Hollom's example on its own cannot disprove the original conjecture. Gladkov, Pak and Zimin built a robust graph gadget that imitates a kept or deleted triple closely enough that the failure survives. The price is size: thousands of vertices. Their proof is exact, not a simulation.

## Why it's worth knowing

- **Monotone intuition is not a proof.** Correlation inequalities (Harris–FKG, van den Berg–Kesten) make many "obvious" percolation statements true. The bunkbed inequality looked like one of them, and it isn't.
- **Small cases can mislead for a long time.** Exhaustive checks like the one above pass. Counterexamples can need a structural idea, here from hypergraphs, rather than more computing.
- **It's UCLA work.** Pak is a professor in the UCLA math department and works on combinatorics and probability, so a talk or seminar on this topic is close at hand.
