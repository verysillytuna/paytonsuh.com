**TL;DR.** Nothing new could be confirmed in the last two days: the AI-assisted claims from late October are still waiting on referees, so they stay out of this entry. Instead, a classic. A **cap set** is a subset of $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ with no three distinct points on a line. For decades the best upper bounds came from Fourier analysis and were only barely smaller than $3^n$. In May 2016 Croot, Lev and Pach proved an exponential bound in $(\mathbb{Z}/4)^n$ with a new polynomial trick, and within weeks Ellenberg and Gijswijt adapted it to $\mathbb{F}_3^n$, showing every cap set has size $o(2.756^n)$. Both papers appeared back to back in *Annals of Mathematics* 185 (2017). The argument fits on a page, and it's below in full, in the "slice rank" form Terence Tao gave it shortly afterwards.

## The question

In $\mathbb{F}_3^n$, three distinct points $x, y, z$ lie on a line exactly when $x + y + z = 0$. (A line is $\{a, a+d, a+2d\}$, and these sum to $3a + 3d = 0$. Conversely, if $x+y+z=0$ then $z = x + 2(y-x)$.) So:

> A set $A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^n$ is a **cap set** if $x + y + z = 0$ with $x, y, z \in A$ forces $x = y = z$.

Equivalently, $A$ has no three-term arithmetic progression. For $n = 4$ this is the card game SET: $81$ cards are the points of $\mathbb{F}_3^4$, a "set" is a line, and the largest table with no set has $20$ cards.

How big can a cap set be? Lower bounds grow like $c^n$ for some $c > 2$, by products of good small examples. For upper bounds, Meshulam (1995) adapted Roth's Fourier argument to get $O(3^n/n)$, and Bateman and Katz (2012) improved this to $O(3^n/n^{1+\varepsilon})$. Whether the answer is $(3 - \delta)^n$ for some $\delta > 0$ was open, and was regarded as hard.

> **Theorem (Ellenberg–Gijswijt, 2016).** Every cap set $A \subseteq \mathbb{F}_3^n$ satisfies $|A| \le 3M_n$, where $M_n$ is the number of vectors $a \in \{0,1,2\}^n$ with $a_1 + \dots + a_n \le 2n/3$. In particular $|A| = o(2.756^n)$.

## The proof

The idea: build one function of three variables that is "diagonal" on $A \times A \times A$, then show it must have large rank (because it's diagonal) and small rank (because it's a low-degree polynomial).

**Step 1: a diagonal function.** In $\mathbb{F}_3$, $1 - t^2$ is $1$ at $t = 0$ and $0$ at $t = \pm 1$. So
$$
\delta(w) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(1 - w_i^2\bigr)
$$
is $1$ at $w = 0$ and $0$ elsewhere on $\mathbb{F}_3^n$. Define $T : A \times A \times A \to \mathbb{F}_3$ by $T(x, y, z) = \delta(x + y + z)$. Since $A$ is a cap set, $T(x,y,z) = 1$ if $x = y = z$ and $0$ otherwise.

**Step 2: slice rank.** Call a function $A^3 \to \mathbb{F}_3$ a *slice* if it has one of the forms $f(x)\,g(y,z)$, $f(y)\,g(x,z)$ or $f(z)\,g(x,y)$. The **slice rank** of $T$ is the least number of slices that sum to $T$. For two variables this is ordinary matrix rank.

**Step 3: a diagonal function has full slice rank.** Claim: $T$ has slice rank $|A|$. (At most $|A|$ is clear: $T = \sum_{a \in A} \mathbf{1}_a(x)\,\mathbf{1}_a(y)\mathbf{1}_a(z)$.) Suppose
$$
T = \sum_{i=1}^{r_1} f_i(x)\,g_i(y,z) + \sum_{j=1}^{r_2} f'_j(y)\,g'_j(x,z) + \sum_{k=1}^{r_3} f''_k(z)\,g''_k(x,y).
$$
The functions $h : A \to \mathbb{F}_3$ with $\sum_z h(z) f''_k(z) = 0$ for every $k$ form a subspace of dimension at least $|A| - r_3$. Any subspace of dimension $m$ contains a vector with at least $m$ nonzero coordinates (put a basis in reduced row echelon form and add the rows: each pivot coordinate is hit by exactly one row). Pick such an $h$ and multiply both sides by $h(z)$, summing over $z$:

- the left side becomes the diagonal matrix $(x, y) \mapsto h(x)\,[x = y]$, whose rank is the number of nonzero entries of $h$, at least $|A| - r_3$;
- the third sum vanishes, and each term of the first two becomes a rank-one matrix, such as $f_i(x) \cdot \sum_z g_i(y,z) h(z)$.

So $|A| - r_3 \le r_1 + r_2$, that is, $r_1 + r_2 + r_3 \ge |A|$.

**Step 4: a low-degree polynomial has small slice rank.** Expand
$$
T(x,y,z) = \prod_{i=1}^{n} \bigl(1 - (x_i + y_i + z_i)^2\bigr)
$$
into monomials $x^a y^b z^c = \prod_i x_i^{a_i} y_i^{b_i} z_i^{c_i}$. Since $t^3 = t$ on $\mathbb{F}_3$, each exponent can be taken in $\{0, 1, 2\}$, and each monomial has total degree $|a| + |b| + |c| \le 2n$, where $|a| = \sum_i a_i$. So in every monomial one of $|a|, |b|, |c|$ is at most $2n/3$. Sort the monomials into three groups by which one (choosing the first, if several are), and collect terms:
$$
T = \sum_{|a| \le 2n/3} x^a\, F_a(y,z) + \sum_{|b| \le 2n/3} y^b\, G_b(x,z) + \sum_{|c| \le 2n/3} z^c\, H_c(x,y).
$$
That's at most $3M_n$ slices.

**Step 5: combine and count.** Steps 3 and 4 give $|A| \le 3M_n$. To bound $M_n$, for any $0 < t \le 1$,
$$
M_n \le \sum_{a \in \{0,1,2\}^n} t^{|a| - 2n/3} = \Bigl(\frac{1 + t + t^2}{t^{2/3}}\Bigr)^{n},
$$
since each term with $|a| \le 2n/3$ is at least $1$. At $t \approx 0.593$ the bracket is about $2.7551$. $\blacksquare$

## Why it's worth knowing

- **Linear algebra beat harmonic analysis.** The Fourier method had been refined for twenty years. The new proof uses only that polynomials of low degree span a small space, and that a diagonal matrix with nonzero diagonal has full rank. Step 3's "largest support in a subspace" move is pure 115AH.
- **The method spread fast.** Within months it gave new bounds for related problems (sunflower-free sets, tri-colored sum-free sets, and progression-free sets in other groups), and slice rank is now a standard tool. Exactly why it works so well, and whether $2.756$ is close to the truth, are still open: the best known constructions are well below it.
- **It's been checked by machine.** Dahmen, Hölzl and Lewis formalized the Ellenberg–Gijswijt proof in the Lean proof assistant in 2019 ([arXiv:1907.01449](https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.01449)).
